"Alert when CPU is above 80%" is how most monitoring starts, and it's where most alert fatigue comes from. A fixed threshold is too sensitive at peak hours and blind at quiet ones. Anomaly detection asks a better question: is this metric behaving differently from how it usually behaves? You don't need an ML platform to answer it. Prometheus can do it with a few recording rules.

What you'll learn: dynamic baselines with z-scores, handling daily and weekly patterns, forecasting problems before they happen, and turning it all into alert rules that don't page you for nothing.

Why static thresholds fail

  • Traffic has rhythm. 500 requests per second may be normal at noon and alarming at 4am, yet a single threshold treats both the same.
  • Services differ. One threshold for 40 services means it's wrong for most of them.
  • Gradual failures hide. A memory leak that grows 2% an hour never crosses a threshold until it's too late.
  • Drops matter too. A sudden fall in traffic (a broken load balancer, a bad DNS change) is often worse than a spike, and "above X" alerts never catch it.

Step 1: Record the base signal

Anomaly queries compare "now" with long windows of history. Computing that from raw counters on every evaluation is expensive, so first record the signal once a minute with a recording rule:

groups:
  - name: anomaly-detection
    interval: 1m
    rules:
      - record: job:http_requests:rate5m
        expr: sum by (job) (rate(http_requests_total[5m]))

Recorded series are cheap to query over long ranges, which is exactly what the next steps need.

Step 2: Dynamic baselines with z-scores

A z-score measures how far the current value is from the recent average, in units of standard deviation. Roughly: |z| < 2 is normal, 2–3 is unusual, and above 3 is rare enough to look at.

      - record: job:http_requests:rate5m:avg_1d
        expr: avg_over_time(job:http_requests:rate5m[1d])

      - record: job:http_requests:rate5m:stddev_1d
        expr: stddev_over_time(job:http_requests:rate5m[1d])

      - record: job:http_requests:rate5m:zscore
        expr: |
          (job:http_requests:rate5m - job:http_requests:rate5m:avg_1d)
            / (job:http_requests:rate5m:stddev_1d > 0)

The > 0 filter on the standard deviation avoids dividing by zero for perfectly flat series (a new service, or one with no traffic yet).

Plot job:http_requests:rate5m:zscore in Grafana with horizontal lines at +3 and −3. You'll see each service's "normal" band without having to pick a threshold for each one.

Step 3: Handle daily and weekly patterns

A 24-hour average blends day and night, so the Monday-morning ramp-up looks like an anomaly every week. Compare with the same time in previous weeks instead:

# current traffic as a ratio of the same time last week
job:http_requests:rate5m
  / job:http_requests:rate5m offset 1w

# more robust: compare with the average of the last three weeks
job:http_requests:rate5m
/ (
    (
        job:http_requests:rate5m offset 1w
      + job:http_requests:rate5m offset 2w
      + job:http_requests:rate5m offset 3w
    ) / 3
  )

A ratio of 1.0 means "the same as usual for this hour and weekday". Below 0.5 or above 2 is worth a look. Averaging several weeks keeps one unusual week (a holiday or an earlier incident) from skewing the baseline.

Retention matters: offset 3w needs at least three weeks of data. Check your Prometheus --storage.tsdb.retention.time, or use long-term storage (Thanos, Mimir) for these queries.

Step 4: Forecast problems before they happen

predict_linear() fits a straight line through recent data and extrapolates it. It's ideal for slow, steady failures:

# disk will be full within 4 hours, based on the last 6 hours
predict_linear(node_filesystem_avail_bytes{fstype!="tmpfs"}[6h], 4 * 3600) < 0

# container memory will hit its limit within 1 hour (memory leak)
predict_linear(container_memory_working_set_bytes{container!=""}[1h], 3600)
  > on (namespace, pod, container)
    kube_pod_container_resource_limits{resource="memory"}

A forecast alert gives you hours of warning instead of minutes, and turns a 3am incident into a ticket for the morning.

Prometheus also has double_exponential_smoothing() (called holt_winters() before Prometheus 3.0) for smoothing noisy series. In Prometheus 3.x it's experimental and must be enabled with the promql-experimental-functions feature flag.

Step 5: Turn it into alerts that don't cry wolf

Raw anomaly scores are noisy. Three guards make them usable:

  1. A minimum volume: going from 0.1 to 0.5 requests per second is a 5× jump, but it doesn't matter. Require real traffic before alerting.
  2. A duration (for:): only alert if the anomaly lasts, not on one odd scrape.
  3. Warning severity: anomalies are hints, so send them to a channel or ticket and keep paging for SLO burn-rate alerts.
      - alert: TrafficAnomaly
        expr: |
          abs(job:http_requests:rate5m:zscore) > 3
          and job:http_requests:rate5m > 1
        for: 10m
        labels:
          severity: warning
        annotations:
          summary: "Unusual traffic for {{ $labels.job }}"
          description: "Request rate is {{ $value | printf \"%.1f\" }} standard deviations from its 24h average."

      - alert: DiskWillFillIn4Hours
        expr: |
          predict_linear(node_filesystem_avail_bytes{fstype!="tmpfs"}[6h], 4 * 3600) < 0
          and node_filesystem_avail_bytes{fstype!="tmpfs"}
            / node_filesystem_size_bytes{fstype!="tmpfs"} < 0.2
        for: 15m
        labels:
          severity: warning
        annotations:
          summary: "Disk on {{ $labels.instance }} ({{ $labels.mountpoint }}) is predicted to fill within 4h"

The second condition on the disk alert (less than 20% free) stops brief write bursts on a mostly empty disk from triggering it.

Step 6: Test your rules

  • Check syntax in CI with promtool check rules anomaly-rules.yml.
  • Unit-test the logic with promtool test rules: you describe input series and the alerts you expect, with no live cluster needed.
  • Backtest by pasting each expression into Grafana over the last 30 days. Count how often it would have fired, and how many of those were real problems.

Where this fits in AIOps

Anomaly detection is level 2–3 of the AIOps maturity model: it catches problems static thresholds miss. Pair it with an LLM triage agent, which gathers context when an anomaly fires, and with SLO burn-rate alerts, which decide when to wake someone up.
